Small-business set-asides explained (SB, 8(a), HUBZone, SDVOSB, WOSB/EDWOSB)

What small-business, 8(a), HUBZone, SDVOSB, and WOSB/EDWOSB set-asides mean, where eligibility is defined by SBA, and why no certification guarantees a contract.

Free guide6 min readSources checked Nov 12, 2026

A set-aside is a federal contract competition that is limited to certain kinds of small businesses. It is a rule about who may compete, not a promise that you will win, and not a certificate that makes a weak bid strong. The contracting officer announces the set-aside on the SAM.gov notice. Your job is to read that field, confirm you actually qualify, and avoid representing a status you do not have.

This guide is a plain-English map to the official sources. It is not a determination of your eligibility. Only SBA’s rules, and in some cases an SBA certification decision, settle that question. BidBeacon is not a government agency and is not affiliated with SBA or SAM.gov.

Small business (SB) set-asides

The most common limitation you will see is a total small-business set-aside: only small businesses may submit an offer. “Small” is not a feeling and is not “fewer than 500 employees” in every industry. SBA sets a size standard for each NAICS code. For janitorial services (NAICS 561720), the current standard is a receipts test, not an employee cap; see our NAICS 561720 and size-standard guide and confirm the figure in 13 CFR 121.201 or SBA’s current table.

FAR Part 19 is the procurement rulebook for these set-asides. Contracting officers also use the “rule of two” when deciding whether to set a purchase aside: if they expect at least two responsible small-business offers at a fair price, they generally should set it aside. That is the government’s decision, not yours.

You typically represent small-business size in SAM.gov and again with your offer. Affiliation matters: if another company controls you, or you control it, its receipts can count toward your size. Getting this wrong is not a paperwork typo; SBA describes serious penalties for knowing misrepresentation.

8(a) Business Development

The 8(a) program is an SBA business-development program for small businesses owned and controlled by socially and economically disadvantaged U.S. citizens, and for certain entity-owned firms (for example, some tribal, ANC, NHO, and CDC-owned companies). SBA, not the contracting officer and not a paid consultant, certifies participants. SBA’s current overview states that firms generally must already be small, meet ownership and disadvantage tests, show good character, and demonstrate potential for success (often including a two-year business history, with some waivers). Participation is time-limited (SBA describes a nine-year term with development and transitional stages).

8(a) firms may be eligible for competitive 8(a) set-asides and, in some situations, sole-source awards under SBA and FAR 19.8 rules. Certification does not guarantee any award. SBA says so directly. If you think 8(a) might fit, start with SBA’s certifications page and apply only through official SBA channels, after a conversation with an SBA district office or an APEX Accelerator counselor.

HUBZone

The HUBZone program is for small businesses that meet principal-office and employee-residency tests in a Historically Underutilized Business Zone, plus ownership and control rules (U.S. citizens or certain specified entities). SBA certifies HUBZone firms and maintains the official HUBZone map. Map boundaries change; do not rely on a memory of last year’s census tract.

HUBZone-certified firms can compete for HUBZone set-asides and, in full-and-open competitions, may receive a price-evaluation preference that SBA currently describes as 10 percent. Recertification and continuing eligibility rules apply. Check 13 CFR Part 126 and SBA’s program page for the current tests before you apply.

SDVOSB (service-disabled veteran-owned)

Service-disabled veteran-owned small business set-asides are limited to firms SBA has certified as SDVOSBs (and that remain small under the applicable NAICS). Ownership and control generally must rest with one or more veterans whom the Department of Veterans Affairs has determined to have a service-connected disability, under the detailed rules SBA administers through Veteran Small Business Certification (VetCert). The official application portal is veterans.certify.sba.gov.

Do not treat an old self-certification, a state veteran-business certificate, or a logo as a substitute for the current federal certification the notice requires. Read the solicitation and SBA’s veteran certification pages.

WOSB and EDWOSB

The Women-Owned Small Business Federal Contract program lets agencies set aside certain work for certified WOSBs, and some of that work for economically disadvantaged WOSBs (EDWOSBs). SBA’s current eligibility outline is:

  • For WOSB: the firm is small under SBA size standards; it is at least 51 percent owned and controlled by women who are U.S. citizens; and women manage day-to-day operations and long-term decisions.
  • For EDWOSB: the firm meets the WOSB tests, and the women owners meet SBA’s economic-disadvantage thresholds. SBA currently describes those as personal net worth under $850,000, adjusted gross income of $400,000 or less averaged over the prior three years, and personal assets of $6.5 million or less, with official retirement accounts treated under the regulation. Always read 13 CFR Part 127 and SBA’s page; these numbers can be updated.

WOSB set-asides are used in industries SBA has designated as underrepresented or substantially underrepresented. That list is by NAICS code. Do not assume janitorial work (561720) is, or is not, on the current list — check SBA’s eligible-industries list and the NAICS on the notice. Firms must be certified through SBA Certifications (third-party certificates, if you use one, still have to be submitted there). Certification, again, does not guarantee a contract.

How this shows up on a janitorial notice

On a SAM.gov opportunity you will usually see a set-aside field such as Total Small Business, 8(a), HUBZone, SDVOSB, WOSB, EDWOSB, or unrestricted. Combined or partial set-asides also exist. Read that field, the NAICS, and the instructions together. If a notice is an 8(a) set-aside and you are not an 8(a) participant, you generally cannot compete as a prime, even if you are a small cleaning company.

A set-aside also does not relax the statement of work, wage determinations, or past-performance requests. It only changes the competitive pool.

Practical cautions

  • Represent only statuses you can defend. If you are unsure, ask SBA or an APEX counselor before you submit an offer.
  • Keep SAM.gov and any SBA certification records consistent and current.
  • Program rules, maps, and economic-disadvantage thresholds change. Re-check official pages when you apply and when you bid.
  • No reputable counselor will sell you a guarantee. Free help exists.
