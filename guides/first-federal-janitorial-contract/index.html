Winning your first federal janitorial contract: a realistic path

A plain-English path to your first federal janitorial bid: free SAM.gov registration, size and set-asides, reading the notice, site visits, and where to get no-cost help.

Free guide7 min readSources checked Nov 12, 2026

Federal janitorial contracts can look like a shortcut to steady work. Some of them are a good fit for a small cleaning company. Many are not, and almost none of them are won by filling out a form and hoping. This guide is a realistic path for a small contractor who wants to try the official process — not a promise that you will win work, and not legal, tax, or bid-preparation advice.

The official marketplace for federal contract opportunities is SAM.gov. Searching it and registering there is free. Private tools, including BidBeacon, are optional helpers. They are not a substitute for the official notice, and BidBeacon is not affiliated with SAM.gov, GSA, SBA, or any other government agency.

What “winning a first contract” usually actually means

A first award, when it happens, often looks smaller and more local than the huge base-wide janitorial contracts you may have heard about — a ranger district, an FAA facility, a single federal building. Those jobs still require a complete, on-time response and the ability to perform. They are not practice rounds.

SBA’s “Get started with contracting” guidance is blunt: it can take a long time to win a first government contract, and you should have enough cash flow to keep operating while you learn the process. SBA also notes that keeping private-sector customers can help. Those are general SBA observations, not janitorial-specific averages, and not a BidBeacon estimate.

A realistic first-year goal is to get registered correctly, learn to read a notice, bid only where you can actually perform, and use free counseling so you do not miss a mandatory form or a site visit.

Step 1: Confirm the work is janitorial, and that you are “small”

Federal buyers classify work with a NAICS code. Janitorial services are 561720. Facilities support is a different code (561210) and is used when the contract is really a bundle of services, not just cleaning. The contracting officer, not the bidder, assigns the NAICS code on the solicitation. You can be a janitorial company and still see a related code on a particular notice — read that notice, not just the title.

Whether you qualify as a small business for a set-aside is determined by SBA size standards, which vary by NAICS code, and by affiliation rules. For 561720, the current SBA size standard in 13 CFR 121.201 and in SBA’s current table of size standards (effective March 17, 2023, and still the current file on SBA Open Data as of this writing) is $22 million in average annual receipts. Receipts are generally averaged over the last five completed fiscal years and must include affiliates. Confirm the figure yourself with SBA’s Size Standards Tool or the table before you represent that you are small. A more complete walkthrough is in our guide to NAICS 561720, size standards, and SAM registration.

Step 2: Get a Unique Entity ID and register in SAM.gov (free)

You need a Unique Entity ID (UEI) to bid on federal work. You obtain it by registering in SAM.gov, or, in limited cases, by requesting a UEI without a full registration if you only need the identifier. For almost every janitorial prime contract, you want a complete, active entity registration. There is no fee to register on SAM.gov. You will use Login.gov to sign in. After registration, keep the record active; expired registrations are a common, avoidable reason a bid cannot be accepted.

Registering does not place you on a hidden list of “approved cleaners.” It makes you eligible to submit an offer when a solicitation is open and you meet its rules.

Step 3: Decide which set-asides, if any, you can honestly claim

Some solicitations are set aside for small businesses generally, or for certified 8(a), HUBZone, service-disabled veteran-owned (SDVOSB), or women-owned (WOSB/EDWOSB) small businesses. Others are full and open. Eligibility is specific, and several of those programs require SBA certification — not just a box checked in SAM. Self-representing that you qualify when you do not can have serious consequences.

Do not assume you are in a program, and do not assume a set-aside means less competition or a win. Read small-business set-asides explained and confirm current rules on SBA.gov and in FAR Part 19. APEX Accelerator counselors can help you review what you may apply for. They cannot certify you, and neither can BidBeacon.

Step 4: Watch the official notices — and read them completely

New work appears as a contract opportunity on SAM.gov. Notice types are not all the same. A sources-sought or pre-solicitation is often market research; a solicitation or combined synopsis/solicitation is usually the document you respond to. The response date includes a time and a time zone. Amendments can change the statement of work, the wage determination, or the due date. Points of contact, attachments, and site-visit instructions live in the notice, not in a summary.

Our guide on how to read a SAM.gov contract opportunity notice walks through those fields. Until you are comfortable, open the official notice every time and work from the attachments.

Step 5: Go to the site visit if one is offered

Janitorial work is site-specific: access hours, security, dumpster locations, fixtures, and staffing all show up in a walkthrough. FAR 52.237-1, when used, urges offerors to inspect the site and says that failing to inspect is not grounds for a claim after award. If the notice requires you to RSVP or attend in order to bid, treat that as mandatory. See site visits and pre-bid walkthroughs for janitorial bids.

Step 6: Price the work you actually saw, not the work you imagine

Read the performance work statement, quality standards, and any Service Contract Act wage determination in the attachments. Your price has to cover labor at those wages (if they apply), supplies, supervision, insurance, and the contract’s reporting and security rules. If you cannot staff the site at the required times, do not bid it.

Step 7: Submit exactly what the solicitation asks for, on time

Follow the instructions in the official notice: format, email versus portal, page limits, and required representations. Late is usually late. Keep a copy of what you sent and of any confirmation.

Where to get free help

APEX Accelerators (formerly PTACs) provide no-cost counseling on SAM registration, certifications, and bid preparation. They serve businesses in their states. Find an office through the official locator at apexaccelerators.us or the NAPEX directory. SBA district offices and SBA resource partners can also help you understand size and certification programs. None of those offices, and none of the guides on this site, can guarantee a contract.

A first-90-days checklist (process, not a promise)

  1. Confirm your primary NAICS and run SBA’s Size Standards Tool, including affiliates.
  2. Complete a free SAM.gov entity registration and write down your UEI.
  3. Bookmark SAM.gov search for NAICS 561720 and the places of performance you can actually reach.
  4. Read one full solicitation end-to-end, including amendments and attachments, without bidding it if you are not ready.
  5. Talk to your state APEX Accelerator before you apply for a certification or submit a first offer.
  6. Bid only when you can attend any required walkthrough, meet the due date, and staff the building.

That path is slower than a sales pitch, and it stays aligned with the official rules.
